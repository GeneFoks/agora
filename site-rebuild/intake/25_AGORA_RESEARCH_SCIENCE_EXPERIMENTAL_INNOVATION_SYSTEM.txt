AGORA RESEARCH, SCIENCE & EXPERIMENTAL INNOVATION SYSTEM
Version: 1.0 | Working strategic blueprint | October 2026

STATUS AND PURPOSE
This is a design proposal, not a representation that any laboratory, partnership, grant, permit, or scientific result currently exists. It defines how Agora can become a disciplined, open-to-scrutiny living laboratory, beginning with a modest site near Austin, Texas. Decisions involving land use, safety, human participants, animals, regulated equipment, or grants require independent expert and legal review.

01. CENTRAL THESIS
Agora's distinctive research asset is not an expensive laboratory: it is the opportunity to test integrated human settlement systems in real conditions. Research should improve the safety, affordability, resilience, environmental performance, and human experience of settlements. Scientific integrity outranks promotional narratives.

02. MISSION
Design, measure, replicate, and openly evaluate practical systems for regenerative settlements. Produce evidence that others can independently reproduce. Turn validated findings into operational procedures, education, products, and responsibly licensed technology.

03. FOUR RESEARCH HORIZONS
H1, 2026–2027: Agora-01 living laboratory: baseline measurements and low-cost operational experiments.
H2, 2027–2030: replicable pilots at several settlement sizes, with standardized metrics.
H3, 2030–2050: multi-site research network, shared datasets, rigorous comparisons and partnerships.
H4, 2050–2100+: long-duration resilience, knowledge preservation, extreme-environment settlements and, if feasible, off-world applications.
Future horizons are aspirations, not funding or technical commitments.

04. RESEARCH PRINCIPLES
Start with a falsifiable question. Establish a baseline. Define success and failure before testing. Minimize harm and cost. Separate observation from causal inference. Keep raw data traceable. Publish negative results when possible. Avoid exaggerated claims. Never use community members as unwitting research subjects.

05. FOUNDING ROLES AND ACCOUNTABILITY
Eugene and Gennady Fokin are proposed 50/50 founding partners in the founder-controlled commercial architecture. Research governance must clearly distinguish founder business decisions, independent scientific review, and any legally independent nonprofit functions. Research claims require named owners and evidence; founder status does not substitute for scientific expertise.

06. ORGANIZATIONAL MODEL
Agora Research Office: project intake, protocols, records and dashboards.
Research Leads: accountable domain specialists.
Independent Advisors: challenge assumptions and review methods.
Safety/Compliance Review: veto unsafe or unauthorized experiments.
Community Liaison: consent, accessibility, complaints and benefit sharing.
Commercialization Team: evaluate only results supported by evidence.
At launch these can be part-time roles; do not imply a staffed institute exists.

07. RESEARCH PORTFOLIO — SEVEN TRACKS
A. Water and wastewater efficiency.
B. Energy, storage, thermal comfort and demand management.
C. Food systems, soil health and ecological restoration.
D. Housing, construction, materials and maintenance.
E. Human well-being and community design.
F. AI, sensors, robotics and Agora OS.
G. Resilience, knowledge preservation and disaster readiness.
Every track must define a practical Agora-01 benefit.

08. AGORA-01 AS A TESTBED
The proposed approximately 18-acre property, possibly including a lake, is a candidate, not a confirmed permitted research campus. Prior to field experiments verify ownership/closing, zoning, ETJ/city jurisdiction, floodplain, wetlands, water rights, septic requirements, utilities, fire access, environmental constraints and insurance. Protect the lake and habitat from disturbance pending regulatory review.

09. EXPERIMENT SELECTION SCORECARD
Score 0–5 for: safety; regulatory simplicity; resident benefit; measurable outcome; cost; replicability; learning value; implementation speed. Apply a mandatory STOP gate for unresolved safety, consent or permit issues. Select high-scoring projects rather than visually impressive prototypes.

10. FIRST TEN LOW-COST EXPERIMENT CANDIDATES
1) Submeter electrical usage by activity and lodging type.
2) Monitor indoor temperature, humidity and CO2 in occupied units with consent.
3) Compare drip irrigation schedules in raised beds.
4) Track water use per overnight guest and per resident.
5) Trial composting of appropriate plant waste where allowed; exclude unsafe waste streams.
6) Compare shade treatments for summer thermal comfort.
7) Measure soil infiltration and organic matter before and after regenerative practices.
8) Track preventive maintenance against equipment failures.
9) Test inventory labeling and reuse workflows for salvaged construction materials.
10) Evaluate offline-first incident reporting and maintenance workflows in Agora OS.
None requires exposing participants to experimental drugs, unsafe electrical systems, or unapproved waste treatment.

11. STANDARD EXPERIMENT TEMPLATE
Experiment ID; problem; hypothesis; site; owner; partners; legal/safety gates; informed consent (if applicable); baseline; control/comparison; intervention; measurements; sampling frequency; duration; budget; stopping rules; data retention; analysis method; results; limitations; replication decision.

12. SCIENTIFIC QUALITY LADDER
Level 0: anecdote or idea.
Level 1: documented observation.
Level 2: repeatable measurement with baseline.
Level 3: controlled comparison and predeclared criteria.
Level 4: independent replication or specialist review.
Level 5: validated across multiple sites and operating conditions.
Agora communications must state the achieved level; never call an early prototype 'scientifically proven.'

13. HUMAN PARTICIPANT PROTECTION
Health, psychological, behavioral, biometric, or educational studies may constitute human-subjects research. Determine whether ethics review or institutional review board oversight is required before starting. Participation must be informed, voluntary, revocable where applicable, and not a condition of housing, employment or membership. Avoid collecting sensitive data unless necessary. Provide accessible complaint and withdrawal channels.

14. HEALTH AND LONGEVITY BOUNDARY
Agora may offer evidence-informed wellness programs, but must not present unvalidated interventions as treatment or conduct clinical trials without appropriate approvals, clinical oversight and safeguards. No experimental psychedelic or other controlled-substance research without lawful authorization and institutional protections. Measure non-sensitive aggregate operational outcomes first.

15. ANIMAL AND ECOLOGY SAFEGUARDS
Animal-related work requires welfare standards, veterinary consultation and applicable approvals. Wildlife monitoring should minimize disturbance. No unpermitted releases, invasive species trials, excavation, lake filling, chemical applications or wetland alteration. Ecological restoration should begin with surveys and local expert input.

16. DATA ARCHITECTURE
Agora OS stores experiment metadata, timestamped observations, sensor provenance, calibration records, protocol versions and audit trails. Separate personally identifying data from operational datasets. Use least-privilege access, backups, retention limits, documented deletion processes and incident response. A digital twin is a model with uncertainty, not a perfect representation of reality.

17. DATA OWNERSHIP AND CONSENT
Define contracts for data produced by employees, volunteers, guests, residents, vendors and university partners. No blanket claim that all resident-generated information belongs to Agora. Specify licenses, publication permissions, privacy expectations, deletion rights and security responsibilities before collection.

18. OPEN SCIENCE AND INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY
Default to publishing methods, non-sensitive findings and safety lessons. Consider open-source software or open hardware where strategically appropriate. Patent or protect genuinely novel inventions only after legal assessment. Commercial IP owned by founder entities must not be quietly transferred from an independent nonprofit; use documented, fair-value, conflict-reviewed arrangements.

19. UNIVERSITY AND INDUSTRY PARTNERSHIPS
Potential partner categories: Texas universities, extension services, community colleges, local makerspaces, utilities, agricultural specialists and engineering firms. Approach with a one-page problem statement, site status, available data, risk assessment, proposed deliverable and defined contribution. No partnership is assumed until written agreement.

20. INDEPENDENT RED TEAM
For each major project seek critiques from systems engineering, architecture, agronomy, public health, environmental science, economics, law and operations. AI systems (including Claude, Gemini and ChatGPT) can generate alternative hypotheses, but their agreement is not independent empirical validation. Record disagreements and decisions.

21. RESEARCH FUNDING STACK
Phase 1: founder-funded experiments using affordable sensors and volunteer expertise with clear agreements.
Phase 2: revenue-funded applied R&D from legitimate commercial operations.
Phase 3: grants, donations and sponsored studies only when eligibility, governance and restrictions are met.
Phase 4: licensing, consulting, education and product revenues after validation.
Keep restricted charitable funds segregated; no promises of returns to donors.

22. INDICATIVE MICRO-BUDGET
Illustrative first-year research envelope, subject to the separate ~$25,000 first-year site development budget: $300–800 basic sensors/meters; $100–400 calibration and test supplies; $100–300 soil/water laboratory tests; $0–300 data hosting; $200–600 safety materials and training; $0–500 contingency. Total illustrative $700–2,900, NOT approved spending. Prioritize essentials such as safe sanitation, fire protection and lawful occupancy before R&D purchases. Research must not consume money required for minimum viable settlement safety.

23. RESEARCH ECONOMICS
For each project calculate setup cost, ongoing cost, labor hours, measured savings, failure risk, time to payback and sensitivity to occupancy or weather. Distinguish research value from direct ROI. Stop experiments that are unsafe or persistently uninformative.

24. PUBLICATION STANDARD
Each public result should include: question, dates, site conditions, sample size, methodology, baseline, outcome, uncertainty, limitations, financial support and conflicts of interest. Separate aspirational concept renderings from measured results. Publish corrections prominently.

25. CITIZEN SCIENCE MODEL
Residents can help record soil observations, biodiversity sightings, energy logs and maintenance issues. Provide training, optional participation and feedback about findings. Do not pressure residents to contribute unpaid labor or disclose private health information. Compensate specialist work fairly where appropriate.

26. EDUCATION INTEGRATION
Convert validated experiments into workshops, field demonstrations, short technical notes and practical courses. Teach measurement literacy, safe tool use, engineering tradeoffs and critical thinking. Educational programming must not claim accreditation or certifications it does not possess.

27. AGORA KNOWLEDGE ARK
Preserve field manuals, schematics, validated protocols, maintenance histories, open datasets, seed and ecological knowledge, and essential scientific references with appropriate rights. Use redundant offline copies, durable formats, checksums, versioning and periodic restoration drills. Document what was tested and what remains speculative.

28. PROCUREMENT AND MATERIALS
Reuse construction materials only after inspection, traceability and code compliance. Salvaged cables, pipes or fencing may be suitable for some projects but must not be assumed safe for potable water, load-bearing structures, electrical installations or public facilities. Record provenance and rejection decisions.

29. FACILITY EVOLUTION
Stage 0: locked storage, field notebooks, laptop, low-cost instruments.
Stage 1: weatherproof bench and basic calibration station.
Stage 2: permitted workshop with ventilation, electrical protection and safety protocols.
Stage 3: specialized partner facilities for regulated tests.
Do not construct or advertise a laboratory before land-use and building approvals.

30. SAFETY STOP RULES
Immediately stop for injury, near-miss, fire risk, electrical hazards, contaminated water, privacy breach, animal distress, ecological damage, unexpected chemical exposure, or missing consent/authorization. Preserve records, secure the area, notify appropriate authorities as required and obtain competent review before restarting.

31. 100-DAY RESEARCH ROADMAP
Days 1–15: establish experiment registry; assign owners; confirm land status and risk gates.
Days 16–30: select 3 safe, inexpensive baseline studies; document protocols.
Days 31–45: acquire only essential measurement tools; verify calibration.
Days 46–70: gather baseline observations; do not rush to claim effects.
Days 71–85: run small controlled improvements where legally and operationally ready.
Days 86–100: publish internal results, costs, failures, uncertainties and next decisions.
If land closing or permits slip, conduct desk research and off-site prototypes instead.

32. FIRST THREE PRIORITY PILOTS
Pilot A — Water Ledger: record source, storage, use and loss, aiming to identify avoidable waste without compromising sanitation.
Pilot B — Thermal Comfort: compare shade, ventilation and insulation approaches in lawful occupied spaces using safe sensors.
Pilot C — Reuse Inventory: create a tagged material catalog, acceptance criteria and avoided-purchase accounting.
These pilots are chosen for affordability, operational usefulness and low regulatory complexity.

33. CORE METRICS
Research throughput: protocols registered, completed and replicated.
Integrity: percentage with baseline and documented limitations.
Safety: incidents and near misses, with severity and corrective action.
Impact: verified water/energy savings, maintenance downtime, material reuse, ecological indicators.
Community: voluntary participation, satisfaction and complaints resolved.
Economics: cost per experiment and validated savings or revenue.
Learning: number of practical procedures improved by evidence.
Avoid vanity metrics such as number of AI-generated ideas.

34. DECISION GATES
Gate A — Is the activity legal and safe on this parcel?
Gate B — Is the hypothesis measurable with available resources?
Gate C — Are consent, privacy and ownership addressed?
Gate D — Does the test improve settlement operations or transferable knowledge?
Gate E — Do results justify replication, modification or termination?
A failed gate means pause, redesign or reject.

35. SCALE-UP TO AGORA NETWORK
Create a common schema for climate, site size, occupancy, construction type, utilities and intervention data. Compare like with like; do not infer universal results from one Texas property. Standardize manuals, sensor calibration, safety gates and reporting so future Agora-25, Agora-100, Agora-500, Agora-5000 and Agora-City sites can replicate studies.

36. EXTERNAL IMPACT AND COMMERCIALIZATION
Potential outputs include field-tested settlement design templates, open technical guides, Agora OS modules, training curricula, consulting services and licensed devices. Require evidence of reliability, clear warranties and compliance before sale. Do not monetize private resident data without a lawful, transparent and genuinely consensual basis.

37. FAILURE MODES
Science theater: flashy demonstrations without controls.
Founder bias: favorable findings rewarded, failures suppressed.
Regulatory overreach: experiments launched before permits.
Privacy erosion: excessive resident monitoring.
Grant capture: projects optimized for funding rather than need.
Tech debt: sensors installed without maintenance ownership.
Mission drift: research displaces basic housing and sanitation.
Mitigation: preregistration, independent challenge, transparent budgets, stop rules and operational priorities.

40. DEFINITION OF SUCCESS
Within the first operational year, Agora-01 has a small set of safely executed, documented experiments that change actual decisions. Success is not a 'world-class lab' label. It is reliable learning, honest evidence, safer operations, measurable improvements and protocols others can repeat.
